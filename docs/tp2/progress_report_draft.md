## 2. Working prototype: the agentic AI component (12 pts): Shu
- **Design:** 7 tools, all built and tested (#60): `fetch_sec_filings`, `find_ai_passages`, `classify_passage` (the only one that calls the model), `verify_label` (the guardrail), `score_companies`, `lookup_baseline` (Dong et al.), `get_labels`. No transcript tool yet: Bloomberg use is cleared (#62), but the team hasn't decided whether transcripts go in the index.
- **Oct 28 target met:** runs end to end on the six FY2025 10-Ks plus Citi (110 passages): find AI passages → classify → verify the quote → score → CSV for the dashboard (#66, #68, #84).
- **Model:** DeepSeek `deepseek-flash` (temperature 0, fixed JSON schema, answers cached). Funding decided Oct 25 (#55): one person pays; all runs so far cost about $0.30. Only public SEC text is sent.
- **Evaluated:** on the 50-passage labeling sample (#85), on the 29 passages never used for tuning the agent matched the human majority on 24 of the 25 that had one (96%), with no overclaims (0–2% across runs); the guardrail held back 2 labels that would have been overclaims.

The agent reads the AI passages in SEC filings, labels each one, and turns the labels into company scores. Only `classify_passage` calls the model; every label then passes a rule-based guardrail (`verify_label`): the quote must appear word for word in the filing, a "specific" label needs its detail inside the quote, and labels under 0.70 confidence go to a person. Batch mode labels passages in a fixed order and writes the files the dashboard reads; chat mode lets the model pick its own tools to answer a question, and every claim must cite a quoted passage. Example output, for Citi's FY2025 10-K (`C_2025-12-31_10-K_00131`): specific use (D2) in operations and reporting, quoting "applied technology solutions leveraging AI to support governance of data reported in key regulatory reports", confidence 0.90, guardrail passed. Code and demo: `src/agent/` (`run_batch.py`, `chat.py`, `redteam.py`), design in `docs/part6-agent/agent_design.md`; `python3 -m src.agent.chat --trace "How does Citigroup use AI in 2025?"` shows each tool call and the cited answer (add `AGENT_LLM_PROVIDER=mock` to run it offline).

*Snehal:* I designed and built the Streamlit dashboard to pair a real-data research view with a separate envisioned interface. The SEC pilot scores tab reads our scorer's company-year output and summarizes AI Use Score, readiness, and talk ratio across firms and years; the values are labeled preliminary because they come from SEC disclosures scored with AI-assisted v0 labels, not verified measures of firm-wide AI adoption. The firm-story and comparison tabs remain clearly marked fictional examples to demonstrate how researchers could explore use cases without presenting mock records as evidence. ## 7. Evaluation and responsible AI (supports sections 1–3): Mehul and Logan
*Draft (Claude, from the repo; Mehul to check):* Three teammates labeled the same 50 passages independently. On the core question, real use or not, agreement met our bar (Cohen's κ 0.60–0.72 across pairs). On the five evidence levels it didn't (Fleiss κ 0.27), mostly because one rule was read two ways. We clarified it and ran round 2 on 30 new passages chosen from the hard cases ([results](../part3-metrics/labeling_round_2/results_round2.md)). The rule we fixed no longer causes disagreement, but agreement is still below the bar: five levels κ 0.32 (closest pair 0.67), and real use or not 0.45–0.71. The main gaps are now hedged risk sentences read as plans and sentences on the line between use and talk. On the 29 passages it hadn't seen during tuning, the agent matched the human majority on 24 of the 25 that had one (96%, κ 0.94). Its overclaim rate (saying a company uses AI when people said it doesn't) was 0–2% across runs, versus about 5% without our code-based guardrail (exact-quote check, specific-detail rule, human review below 0.7 confidence). A live run of a 22-case test set (fake quotes, a tampered number, injected instructions, paywalled sources, investment advice) with the guardrail on and off had no unsafe outputs with it on and no wrongly blocked correct answers; without it, 2 of the 18 unsafe cases (the tampered outputs) were accepted as scored labels, and the model handled the other 16 on its own ([check 6](../part3-metrics/check6_results.md)). The three raters then agreed a label for every disputed passage (Nov 7). Rescoring with these 80 human labels in place of the AI drafts raised several firms (Capital One from 3 to 20, Principal from 0 to 18): the drafts had missed use or plans in 18 of the 80, which tells us the draft-based scores understate use. The ranking barely moves when the weights change (Spearman ρ 0.94–0.99). A word-count benchmark (Dong et al.) follows how much firms *talk* about AI (ρ 0.78) more closely than our use score (ρ 0.58); with six firms that is only a direction ([check 4](../part3-metrics/check4_results.md)).

Our search keeps precision at 98.9%, and a sweep of the 132,998 unflagged paragraphs plus a 100-paragraph near-miss sample found no clear miss; robo-advice, described without the word AI, is a known gap ([check 1](../part3-metrics/check1/results.md)). A worst-case replay agrees: the guardrail stops invented quotes and low-confidence claims with no false blocks on correct answers, but can't catch a confident misreading of a real quote, which is why we keep measuring against people ([check 6](../part3-metrics/check6_replay.md)). A teammate who didn't build the pipeline redownloaded everything and got the same 277 passages and identical scores ([check 5](../part3-metrics/check5_rerun.md)).
